{\LARGE\bfseries Dilemma Dynamics}\par
{\large Cooperation, deliberation and repeated-game incentives}\par
\vspace{5pt}
Alisa Sheinkman\par
{\small Evaluation and mathematical notes}
\end{center}
\begin{abstract}
Does drafting before acting help an AI recognise when cooperation pays?
We compare Direct and bounded Draft procedures in Nemotron Nano and Super
across 7,104 repeated Prisoner's Dilemma episodes at temperatures 0 and 1.
Exact payoff references against disclosed policies separate decision quality
from cooperation between autonomous agents. Draft reduces average benchmark
loss by 16.8--37.5\% across four planned comparisons, yet increases loss against
Tit-for-Tat and reduces self-play cooperation. These results connect individual
payoff decisions to the dynamics of reciprocity, and motivate experiments on
contingent planning, relationship repair and richer interactions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and overview}
AI agents may interact repeatedly with people or other agents while pursuing
their own objectives. Their choices can influence whether others cooperate,
retaliate or rebuild a productive relationship. Understanding how individual
decision quality relates to cooperation is therefore useful when evaluating
agents that interact over time.

Repeated Prisoner's Dilemma makes this question concrete: immediate rewards
compete with future consequences. Dilemma Dynamics combines exact payoff
comparisons against disclosed opponent policies with autonomous pairings that
reveal how cooperation develops, breaks down and recovers. Researchers and
agent developers can inspect responses to incentives; students can explore the
same ideas through play, saved recordings and mathematical explanations.

\textbf{Intellectual setting.} Rasmusen's account of repeated games, commitments
and best responses supplies the theoretical foundation \cite{rasmusen}.
Akata et al. study social chain-of-thought in repeated games \cite{akata};
Fontana et al. show model-specific responses to opponent hostility \cite{fontana};
Pal et al. examine strategies across histories and game settings \cite{pal}.
Taylor and Bergen connect reasoning and action--message incongruence in
communication games \cite{taylor}. Together, these works motivate separating
payoff competence, cooperation and communication. Here the scientific protocol
uses sealed actions and asks how a bounded draft changes decisions and interaction.

\section{Game, procedures and evaluation}
\subsection{Incentives and the two procedures}
Players maximise expected own cumulative points. Both choose privately from
cooperate (C) and defect (D), then reveal together. The matrix lists
(own points, other points):
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Own action & Other cooperates & Other defects\\
\midrule
Cooperate & $(3,3)$ & $(0,5)$\\
Defect & $(5,0)$ & $(1,1)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Thus temptation $T=5$, reward $R=3$, punishment $P=1$ and sucker's payoff
$S=0$ satisfy $T>R>P>S$. Defection pays more in one round; a responsive
partner can make cooperation valuable over repeated encounters.

An \emph{episode} is one complete interaction. A fixed horizon announces its
length $H$; a geometric horizon continues independently after each round with
probability $\delta$, giving $\E[N]=1/(1-\delta)$ rounds and no announced last
round. For example, $\delta=.9$ gives ten expected rounds, a different
information setting from ten announced rounds.

The hosted models are NVIDIA Nemotron Nano and Super \cite{nano,super,cards}.
\emph{Direct} makes one thinking-off action request, capped at 128 completion
tokens. \emph{Draft} makes a thinking-on draft capped at 1,024 tokens, followed
by a separate thinking-off action request capped at 128 tokens. Both receive
completed history; only the current draft is added to its action request.
An \emph{arm} is a model--procedure combination. Each episode uses temperature
$\tau\in\{0,1\}$ throughout, with top-$p=1$. The comparison concerns the whole
two-stage procedure, including its added computation and conditioning.

\subsection{Two complementary tracks}
\textbf{Track A: disclosed opponents.} Always Cooperate (AllC), Always Defect
(AllD), Tit-for-Tat (TFT: C first, then copy the subject's preceding action), and
Grim (C until a subject defection, then D forever) each face all three continuation
probabilities $.4,.6,.9$. One-round AllC/AllD and announced ten-round TFT/Grim
complete 16 conditions. This benchmark measures decisions against known rules.

\textbf{Track B: autonomous interaction.} All ten unordered pairings of the four
arms, including self-play, interact at $\delta=.9$. Their response policies
emerge during play. This track measures cooperation, joint return and
breakdown/repair trajectories rather than applying the known-policy oracle.

The complete evaluation has \textbf{7,104 episodes}: 6,144 benchmark episodes
and 960 autonomous dyads, with 3,552 episodes at each temperature.
There are \textbf{48 paired blocks per temperature}, balanced across four
neutral-action-label/display-seat strata with 12 blocks each. Every block has
16 benchmark conditions for each arm and ten autonomous pairings. Consequently,
each arm has 768 benchmark episodes per temperature and each autonomous
pairing has 48 dyads. Shared environment draws pair arms and temperatures;
adjacent temperature pairs use frozen random order. Block count was selected
using engineering resource forecasts before scientific outcomes.

\subsection{Exact payoff loss and uncertainty}
For a disclosed opponent, let $s$ be its memory state, $r(s,a)$ the subject's
payoff, and $f(s,a)$ the next state. Exact dynamic programming computes
\begin{equation}
 Q^*(s,a)=r(s,a)+\delta V^*(f(s,a)),\qquad
 V^*(s)=\max_{a\in\{\C,\D\}}Q^*(s,a).
 \label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
Finite games use remaining-horizon values with $V^*_0=0$ and backward induction.
At a revealed decision, the gap $g_t=V^*(s_t)-Q^*(s_t,a_t)\geq0$ measures the
expected own payoff forgone by that action, allowing optimal play afterward.
Episode loss is
\begin{equation}
 L_{\rm geometric}=(1-\delta)\sum_{t=1}^{N}g_t,\qquad
 L_{\rm fixed}=\frac1H\sum_{t=1}^{H}g_t.
 \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Zero means no achievable expected payoff was given up. Survival to a recorded
round already supplies the geometric survival factor; multiplying each saved
gap by $\delta^{t-1}$ again would count stopping twice. Loss has normalised
own-payoff units, and is separate from the amount of cooperation.

Four primary Draft--Direct contrasts average the 16 conditions and four strata
equally. Negative differences favour Draft. Uncertainty uses 50,000 deterministic
stratified whole-block bootstrap draws. Approximate 98.75\% marginal intervals
apply the planned Bonferroni allocation of a 5\% family across four contrasts.
The independent resampling unit is the matched block, not a round. Six planned
temperature contrasts use nominal 95\% intervals without multiplicity correction;
opponent, presentation, planning and self-play diagnostics are exploratory.

\subsection{Temperature, presentation and planning}
Temperature has no uniform preferred setting. Super Direct improves at zero
(0 minus 1: $-.1372$, nominal 95\% interval [$-.1814,-.0942$]); Super Draft
changes little ($-.0022$, [$-.0193,+.0147$]). Nano Direct also changes little
($-.0080$, [$-.0480,+.0316$]), whereas Nano Draft worsens ($+.0513$,
[$+.0199,+.0815$]). The drafting advantage narrows at zero by $+.0592$
[$+.0099,+.1084$] for Nano and $+.1350$ [$+.0876,+.1849$] for Super.

At zero, Nano's exploratory drafting effect reverses with the neutral action
mapping in both seats: $-.4652,-.2558$ when X means C, versus $+.1427,+.1431$
when X means D. The strata also use different stopping draws, motivating a
fully crossed presentation experiment. Equal weighting retains both patterns
in the primary estimate.

Against committed TFT/Grim with ten announced rounds, nine C moves followed
by D earns the optimal 32 points. Only one of 768 episodes follows that path.
Super Draft often makes the useful local TFT endgame choices (43/48 C moves
at round nine and 48/48 D moves at round ten, at zero) while losing payoff
earlier. This distinguishes a useful short plan from planning through the
whole relationship.

\subsection{Self-play separates cooperation from benchmark competence}
Changing both self-play agents from Direct to Draft lowers episode-weighted
mutual cooperation for each model at each temperature (Figure~\ref{fig:self}).
Nano changes from 15.6\% to 10.2\% at zero and 24.6\% to 5.1\% at one;
Super changes from 26.8\% to 0.7\% and from 13.9\% to 2.3\%, respectively.
Each episode contributes its own CC fraction with equal weight. A pooled
round rate weights longer episodes more; rounds within one trajectory share
a history and are dependent.
\begin{figure}[htbp]\centering
\includegraphics[width=.94\linewidth]{../figures/autonomous-self-play.png}
\caption{Mean within-episode mutual cooperation in autonomous self-play.
Each comparison changes both agents; there are 48 dyads per pairing/temperature.
These comparisons are exploratory.}
\label{fig:self}
\end{figure}

Normalised joint return is $J=(1-\delta)\sum_t(r_{1,t}+r_{2,t})$, with
$\delta=.9$. Its mean also falls under Draft in all four self-play comparisons:
Nano $2.7500\to2.5083$ at zero and $3.3729\to2.3854$ at one; Super
$3.2063\to1.8667$ and $3.1500\to2.0792$. This is a geometric-normalised
episode total, rather than a realised per-round average. Each pairing's
schedule contains 376 rounds over 48 dyads (7.833 rounds per dyad).
For Super at zero, the joint-return difference is $-1.3396$, with exploratory
nominal 95\% paired-block interval [$-1.8375,-.9042$].

Repair makes the contrast concrete. Super Direct at zero starts with no
mutually cooperative first rounds, then returns to CC in 82/218 eligible
transitions. Super Draft returns in 0/325 opportunities. The corresponding
\emph{round counts} are 124/376 and 3/376, with different denominators from
the episode-weighted chart. Initial cooperation and the capacity to recover
it are distinct behaviours.

\section{Conclusions and future directions}
A bounded draft improves average exact own-payoff decisions over the balanced
benchmark, while responsive opponents and autonomous play reveal a different
story for reciprocity. Local exploitation, contingent planning and cooperative
repair emerge as separate evaluation targets. The interactive app connects
these aggregate findings to individual decisions and histories, giving
researchers, agent developers and students complementary ways to inspect them.

\paragraph{Planning through responses.}
Compare explicit state-transition plans at different depths, and vary draft
length independently while matching compute. Score first actions and later
continuation choices separately. Crossing every stopping seed with every
label/seat setting would test whether the resulting plans remain stable under
equivalent presentations.

\paragraph{Relationship formation and repair.}
Use controlled forgiving partners to vary when a cooperative probe or return
to cooperation is possible. This would reveal how reciprocal exploration
moves a pair into, maintains, or restores a productive interaction, and when
individually defensive choices sustain low-cooperation patterns.

\paragraph{Communication and noise.}
Add messages alongside controlled action or observation errors. Compare
commitments with subsequent choices and test whether explanations help agents
distinguish mistakes from changed strategies. This links payoff reasoning,
cooperation and commitment reliability in one protocol.

\paragraph{Longer and changing interactions.}
Separate available history from expected remaining interaction, and change a
partner's rule during play. These interventions would distinguish memory and
context use from incentives to preserve a relationship. Repeated encounters
across episodes would make reputation and learning accessible to study.

\paragraph{Diverse incentives and populations.}
Vary temptation, shared surplus and punishment; compare asymmetric roles,
larger groups and mixtures of simple policies, adaptive agents and humans.
Partner selection would then connect the quality of individual decisions to
which relationships and strategies persist across a population.

\section{Incentive calculations and app analysis}
\label{app:incentives}
\subsection{When future responses make cooperation pay}
Against untriggered Grim, cooperation forever earns $R/(1-\delta)$; defecting
now and best-responding to permanent D earns $T+\delta P/(1-\delta)$. Thus
\[
\frac{R}{1-\delta}\geq T+\frac{\delta P}{1-\delta}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\delta\geq\frac{T-R}{T-P}=\frac12.
\]
Against initially cooperating TFT, the three candidate continuation values are
\[
\underbrace{\frac3{1-\delta}}_{\text{always C}},\qquad
\underbrace{5+\frac\delta{1-\delta}}_{\text{always D}},\qquad
\underbrace{\frac5{1-\delta^2}}_{\text{alternate D,C}}.
\]
Comparison makes initial C optimal for $\delta\geq2/3$, explaining why $.6$
and $.9$ reward different patterns. With ten announced rounds against committed
TFT/Grim, backward induction yields nine C moves and a final D, worth
$9R+T=32$. This uses a fixed response rule, rather than the different
finite-game assumption in which both players remain free to optimise
\cite[Sections 4.1 and 5.1--5.2]{rasmusen}.

\subsection{Reported forecasts in the educational app}
Optional app forecasts are separate from the evaluation's action-only requests.
Let $p_t$ be a player's reported probability of the opponent's current C, and
$F_{a,t}$ its expected own total \emph{from the next round onward}, conditional
on continuation and action $a$ now. Define $\rho_t=\delta$ in a geometric game,
$1$ before an announced final round, and $0$ at that final round. Then
\begin{align*}
\widehat Q_t(\C)&=3p_t+\rho_tF_{\C,t},\\
\widehat Q_t(\D)&=1+4p_t+\rho_tF_{\D,t},
\end{align*}
so cooperation is weakly preferred according to those forecasts exactly when
\[
\rho_t(F_{\C,t}-F_{\D,t})\geq1+p_t.
\]
The right side is the immediate temptation to defect; the left side is the
forecast future advantage of C, weighted by continuation. Future totals lie
between zero and $5/(1-\delta)$ in geometric games or $5(H-t)$ in fixed games.
At a known final round, future advantage is zero and D pays more for every
$p_t$. This checks consistency with submitted forecasts, not hidden beliefs;
accuracy is assessed against outcomes actually observed. A one-step alternative
holds the other player's sealed action fixed; longer alternatives need an
explicit response model.

\section{Recreational games}
Tic-tac-toe and Dots \& Boxes are perfect-information mathematical games on
grids or lattices, with visible sequential moves. They have their own scores
and search references. For Tic-tac-toe, minimax from the current player's
perspective has terminal values $+1,0,-1$ and recursion
$G(x)=\max_{a\in A(x)}[-G(f(x,a))]$.

Dots \& Boxes grants another turn after a capture. If $g(x,a)$ is boxes
captured and $W(x)$ is optimal \emph{remaining}-box difference, then
\[
W(x)=\max_{a\in A(x)}[g(x,a)+\sigma(x,a)W(f(x,a))],\qquad
\sigma=\begin{cases}+1,&g(x,a)>0,\\-1,&g(x,a)=0.\end{cases}
\]
At completion $W=0$; previously owned boxes are added separately. The sign
preserves perspective on an extra turn and switches it when play passes.
The app labels incomplete bounded searches unknown and presents chain/loop
references only within their assumptions. These tactics complement the
cooperation study in the separate Recreational games area.
